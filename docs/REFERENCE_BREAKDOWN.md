# Reference breakdown: what the two quality references do, second by second

Sources: `How Mine Salting Worked.mp4` (61.1 s, 720×1280, 30 fps) and `Why Samurai Feared The Flying Thunder.mp4` (59.1 s, 1080×1920, 30 fps).

How this was made: every frame was sampled at 2 fps (and 6–9 fps around the key actions), cuts were found by frame difference, the narration was transcribed with word times by a speech recogniser, and the burnt-in captions were read from the frames and timed against those words. Times are seconds from the start. Narration is quoted in short fragments only, to show where each picture starts.

The second half of this page ("Decisions the pipeline makes") is what the rebuilt pipeline takes from it. The agents read that part; the tables are the evidence.

## The short version

1. **Flat 2D characters live in a real 3D world.** Bold-outlined cartoon figures with white heads, always turned flat to the camera, stand in textured, lit, detailed 3D sets and throw shadows on them. The contrast is the style: simple people, rich world.
2. **The camera travels; it rarely just cuts.** Mine: about 33 distinct framings in 61 s, but only about 14 hard cuts. Samurai: about 22 framings, about 10 hard cuts. The rest are whips, push-ins and follow moves inside one place. A hard cut means the place or the time changed.
3. **The picture arrives with the noun.** A thing is on screen when the narrator names it, usually a few frames before. Each clause gets its own picture or its own action inside the picture.
4. **The opening frame already holds a person, a prop and an action.** No establishing shot first. Mine opens on the man holding the shotgun under a lamp; Samurai opens on the bowling swing with the bomb leaving the hand at 0.4 s.
5. **Objects are followed.** When something is fired, rolled or thrown, the camera goes with it, and the journey reveals the next place and the people who will react.
6. **Every explanation is shown as a mechanism.** Gold flakes swirl in a pan and are then seen flat on a knife under a lens; the bomb opens in mid-air and the fuse is seen wound on its tube next to a spool of thread.
7. **Captions are one or two words, dead centre, on one fixed line.** They change with the voice, about a tenth of a second ahead of it.

## What the references do, by craft

### Script to picture

- **The first picture is the first clause, already in motion.** Both films open on a verb with a person doing it ("loaded", "rolled"). The explanation of who and why comes at 9 s ("You see…") and 3.9 s ("You see…"), after the hook.
- **Nouns pull pictures.** "Shotgun", "gold", "newspaper", "dynamite", "hammer": each is in frame, large, when it is said. When a clause names a new thing, the frame changes to show it; when a clause continues the same action, the frame stays.
- **Things are shown early, never late.** The buddy is passed by the camera at 2.0 s, named at 3.8 s. The dynamite is in the coat lining before "dynamite" is said. Nothing is held back except the twist itself (the rich man's plan), and that is hidden fairly: he simply has not opened his coat yet.
- **A frame stays across several lines when the action inside it is still developing:** the bread-eating (2.4 s), the laugh (4.9 s), the three soldiers from "stops by a fire" to "shakes it" (8 s on one framing, with five separate actions inside it).
- **A cut or insert comes when the action cannot be read at the current size:** a nugget into a barrel, flakes in a pan, a lit fuse, a snapped sword tip, a banknote on a counter.
- **Quoted lines are spoken by a character.** When the narrator voices a character ("So we're shooting gold into a gold mine?", "Get out of my tent!"), that character's mouth moves and the camera is on that face.

### Sets and environments

- **One clear period per film,** carried by five or six kinds of object repeated everywhere: (mine, 1899–1909) timber props, rails, oil lanterns, candle crates with stencilled labels, enamel coffee pot, calendar, bunting, a brass-lamped motor car; (Japan, feudal) paper lanterns, striped war tents, banner poles, armour on a stand, lacquer chests, a rug with a crest.
- **Every set has a light you can see:** a hanging lamp with a visible cone, candles in wall holders, a camp fire, a moon, torches. Faces are lit by them or silhouetted against them.
- **Materials are real and varied:** rock with strata and chips, dusty ground with pebbles, planked walls with gaps, wet metal, cloth. Nothing is a flat colour except the characters.
- **Small readable text sells a place:** "SURE THING MINE EST. 1899", "CANDLES 40 LBS", "NELLIE", a newspaper headline, a calendar month. Text is always in-world and period.
- **Layout serves the action.** The tunnel is a line (so a stick can be thrown down it and a man can run back up it). The camp has a fire as its centre (so a ball can stop "by a fire"). The side chamber has the wall the gold was fired into, the barrel to pan in and the plank wall to stand guilty against, all within a whip-pan of each other.
- **Backgrounds are deep.** Tunnels recede through repeated frames; the camp recedes in rows of tents and fires to mountains; the hillside has a waterfall and trees.

### Camera and editing

- **Three ways to change the picture, in order of use:** travel inside the place (whip, push, follow), cut in or out on the same axis (wide ↔ close of the same subject), and hard cut to a new place or time.
- **Whips are short:** about 0.3 s, 8–10 frames, landing on the new subject as it is named.
- **Push-ins are constant:** almost no framing is truly locked; a held frame drifts in by a few percent.
- **Low camera for people, high for objects.** Characters are seen from about waist height (they look tall and present); pans, counters and floors are seen from above.
- **Lens feel:** wide (strong perspective on rails, tracks, wheels) for places; the characters themselves stay flat and are never distorted.
- **Vertical framing:** the main subject sits between 25% and 60% of the height from the top. The ground and the caption line take the lower third. Tall things (lamp cones, tunnels, banners, the moon) fill the top.
- **Continuity:** screen direction is kept across a sequence (the ball always rolls left to right into the camp; the tunnel always recedes the same way). A character seen at the edge of one frame is the subject of the next.
- **Transitions that are not cuts:** a match on a round shape (bomb over moon → bomb in the tent), dust filling the frame, a flash.

### Character performance

- **A pose is a whole-body shape that reads as a silhouette:** bowling lunge, hands on hips, a kick-back laugh, tiptoe creep with knees high, a flop on the floor.
- **Faces change before bodies and change completely:** flat lids → pin-prick wide eyes → clenched teeth, each held 8–15 frames. Sweat drops and motion ticks are drawn on for fear and effort.
- **Eyes point at the story:** the buddy looks at the wall while eating; the soldiers look at the ball; the rich man looks at us when he laughs.
- **Timing of small things:** an anticipation (wind-up, crouch, raised sword) of 4–8 frames, the action in 2–4 frames, then a hold of 10+ frames on the result. Reactions come 3–6 frames after the cause, and in a group they come one after another, not together.
- **Eating bread (4.0–6.4 s):** seated, bread held at the chest in both hands; the near hand lifts it to the mouth in about 5 frames; the cheek bulges and the mouth works for about 12 frames; the bread comes down with a bite missing (the bread drawing is swapped); repeat. Eyes never leave the wall.
- **Throwing (51.8–52.4 s and 0.0–0.8 s):** the arm goes back, the body leans away, then the whole body swings through and the object leaves on the forward-most frame; the arm stays out for a few frames. The camera leaves with the object.
- **Tossing something back (10.0–12.4 s):** the receiver juggles it first (three quick hand positions), which shows fear better than a face alone.
- **Background characters are alive:** every figure in a wide frame blinks, shifts or reacts.

### Props and object animation

- **Props are 3D and solid; hands are simple dots that sit on them.** Contact is sold by position (the hand is exactly on the stock, the bar, the ball) and by the prop moving with the hand, not by fingers.
- **A prop is introduced in a hand or by a camera move onto it,** never just lying somewhere unremarked, and it is big in frame at least once.
- **Weight:** heavy things make the carrier lean and bounce on landing (ore cart tipping, hammer raised with a back-bend, ball with a small hop and roll-out). Light things spin (hat, sword tip, banknotes, dynamite).
- **Effects mark contact:** sparks on a blade, chips and dust from rock, a light trail on the flying nugget, cracks spreading from a hit, a sparkle on gold.
- **Result stays visible:** the hole with gold in it, the broken swords stuck in the ground, the banknotes on the floor. Later frames keep the evidence of earlier actions.

### Subtitles

- **One or two words at a time** (three at most, for small-word groups like "into the rock"). Articles and prepositions ride with the next word: "the gold", "a pan", "of his".
- **Centred on the frame** to within half a percent, **on one fixed line** at about 70% of the height. The line never moves and never wraps.
- **Bold white rounded sans, heavy black outline, soft shadow.** Sentence case with the script's own punctuation. No colour highlight, no box.
- **Timing:** each caption appears about 0.1–0.2 s before its word is heard and is replaced by the next, so there are no gaps inside a sentence. At a pause the last caption simply stays (the longest is "Completely insane." at 1.4 s).
- **The picture is composed around them.** Faces are above the line; the line usually falls across a body, the ground or a prop.

## Decisions the pipeline makes

These are the rules the rebuilt pipeline applies to any story. Each is enforced or supported by the tool named in brackets.

**Story to picture**

1. Open on the character and the event of the first clause, in motion on frame one. Explain after. [shot-plan: the first view may not be `wide`; `plan-check` warns]
2. Write one *view* per thing the viewer must newly see. A view starts on the first word it illustrates (`on`), says what it `shows` and `why`. A view that lasts over about 6.5 s must list what happens `inside` it. [`plan-check`, `review`]
3. Expect 9–33 views per Short (the reference pace is 22–33 framings a minute) and far fewer hard cuts. Inside a place, reach the next view by travelling (`travel=`) when the eye should be led from one thing to another; cut when place or time jumps or when going in or out on the same subject. [camera.view]
4. Name what the ending depends on as `setups`, and show each as the subject of a view before it pays off. [`plan-check` rejects a payoff that comes before its setup]
5. When the narrator speaks as a character, that character is on screen and its mouth moves. [cast.speak]

**World**

6. One period per film, declared in `world.era`. Every pack character, wardrobe piece, surface and library asset carries period tags and is checked against it. [`plan-check`, assets.use]
7. Build sets around the action: mark where each person stands, where each prop is used, and keep the camera on one side of the action so screen directions hold. [set file MARK table; shot-builder]
8. Give every set a visible light source, real materials and a deep background; dress edges and background, keep acting areas clear. [stagekit.build, lights.rig, mat.surface]

**Performance and props**

9. Stage in poses: anticipation, action, hold. Face first, body after. Eyes on the story. Reactions in turn. [cast.expr / pose / eyes / squash, style guide timings]
10. Hands land exactly on props (`reach`), props travel with hands (`hold`), and leave them cleanly (`release`). Heavy things bend the body; light things spin. Mark contact with a small effect. [cast, fx]
11. Follow thrown, rolled and falling objects with the camera. [camera.follow]

**Captions**

12. One short caption at a time, centred on the frame, one fixed line at 70.5% of the height, never wrapped, small words joined to the next word, appearing 0.08 s before the word. [tools/captions.py, Remotion Captions]
13. Compose faces above the caption line and inside the Shorts safe area. [review draws both; checks flag a covered face]
14. Measure the finished file: caption centre, line stability and safe area from the frames; caption timing against the narration's own pauses, and against recognised speech with `qa --listen`. [tools/qa.py]
